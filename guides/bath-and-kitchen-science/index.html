Bath and kitchen science

Everyday science questions from the bath and the kitchen, each with a short answer from the English episode, a tiny activity you can try at home, and a link to the full episode. Written for parents of curious kids ages 5–10.

We start with the shower questions, then move to the kitchen.

Why do I feel cold right after a hot shower?

After a shower, a thin layer of water stays on your skin. As that water evaporates — turns into a gas called water vapor — it takes heat from your body with it, so you feel cold even though the shower was hot. A fan or a windy day makes this happen faster, because the air blows the vapor away and more water can escape. Drying off with a towel helps, because then there is less water left to steal your heat. Your body uses the same idea when you sweat: the sweat evaporates on purpose to cool you down.

Try this at home

After you wash your hands, dry one hand quickly with a towel and leave the other wet. You can also blow on the wet hand. The wet hand feels colder, because the water on your skin is evaporating and taking heat with it.

Why do bites and cuts feel worse in the shower?

Under the surface of your skin are millions of tiny sensors, like alarm buttons for touch, temperature and pain. A mosquito does not inject “itch juice.” It leaves a tiny drop of saliva, and your body sends out a warning chemical called histamine, which makes those sensors extra sensitive. Warm shower water then triggers them even more, so a bite or a little cut that was quiet all day suddenly itches or stings. When soap stings a cut, that does not mean the soap is cleaning harder; it is just touching nerves that are already on high alert. Scratching can feel good for a moment, but it can also hurt the skin and start the alarm again.

Try this at home

Next shower, if you have a tiny bite or scrape, use lukewarm water instead of a very hot shower, and when you get out, gently pat the skin dry instead of rubbing with the towel. Heat and scrubbing make the alarm feel louder. If a cut looks very red, swollen, or feels hot, tell a grown-up. Do not scratch or rub a wound on purpose just to test it.

Why do we need soap if we already have water?

Oil and water do not mix, so water alone slides right off greasy fingers, like pizza grease, and leaves the oil behind. A soap molecule has two ends: one that likes water and one that likes oil. When you scrub, the grease breaks into tiny drops, and the soap wraps each drop in a packet called a micelle, so the water can wash it away — and the germs that were clinging to that oily dirt go with it. Soap is not a tiny poison that kills every germ. It lifts them off your skin. The foam is mostly a side effect of air getting trapped, and the 20-second wash is simply the time it takes to scrub the hiding places between your fingers and under your nails.

Try this at home

Put a little cooking oil, or a smear of pizza grease, on a fingertip. Rinse with water only: the grease stays. Then soap and scrub for about 20 seconds and rinse again. Now the grease washes off, because the soap can connect the oil to the water.

Why do your fingers get wrinkly in water?

Your fingers do not wrinkle because they soak up water like a sponge. If they did, your stomach and your forehead would wrinkle in the bath too, and they do not. After your hands have been under water for a few minutes, your nervous system notices the water and tells the tiny blood vessels under the skin of your fingers to shrink. With less volume underneath, the skin puckers into wrinkles. Those wrinkles work like a “wet grip mode,” similar to the treads on a tire, so you can hold slippery wet things more safely. When your hands dry, the blood vessels open up again and the wrinkles go away. If a nerve in one finger is injured and cannot send that message, that finger stays smooth even in the water.

Try this at home

Soak just your hands in a bowl of water, or stay in the bath for a few minutes. Then look at your fingers and at your forehead: only the fingers (and usually the toes) wrinkle. Watch them smooth out again as they dry.

How does a microwave heat food so fast?

A microwave oven does not use fire or a blast of hot air. It fills the box with invisible waves that make the tiny water molecules in food spin and bump into each other. That motion creates friction, and the friction creates heat, which is why leftover pizza can get very hot in under a minute. You can feel a smaller version of that friction if you rub your hands together quickly. A ceramic or glass plate may stay cooler than the food because it has little or no water to “dance”; if the plate warms up, it is often just from the hot food sitting on it. The middle of a thick piece can stay cold if it sits in a quieter spot in the box, which is why the plate turns and why the instructions often say to pause and stir.

Try this at home

Rub your hands together fast. They warm up from friction, which is the idea behind what the water molecules in the food are doing. Then, only with a grown-up and a microwave-safe plate, heat a leftover for a short time. Notice that the plate may stay cooler than the food, and that the middle can still be cold, which is why we stir. Never put metal in the microwave, and never run it empty.

How does a refrigerator make my drink cold?

A refrigerator does not make cold. It moves heat out. A special fluid flows through sealed tubes, separate from the food: inside the fridge it picks up heat from the air and from your drink and turns from a liquid into a gas. A compressor squeezes that gas until it is hotter than the kitchen, and the heat flows out through the back or the side panels — that is why those panels feel warm, along with a little extra heat from the electricity that runs the compressor. Then the pressure drops, the fluid can boil and turn into a gas again even while it is very cold, and it is ready to collect more heat inside. Leaving the door open will not cool the kitchen. The heat taken from the open doorway is dumped out the back into the same room, plus the extra heat from the compressor, so the room gets warmer.

Try this at home

With a grown-up, carefully feel the back or the side of the fridge — the outer panels only, not any bare motor or sharp bits. They often feel warm, because that is where the fridge is releasing the heat it took from inside. Do not leave the door open to cool the room: in the episode, that would warm the kitchen.
