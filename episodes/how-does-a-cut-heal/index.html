How Does a Cut Heal?

Listen now
Spotify (opens in a new tab)Apple Podcasts (opens in a new tab)Watch on YouTube (opens in a new tab)Other apps (opens in a new tab)

Ages 5–10 | How does a cut heal? You scrape your knee, and a few days later a scab appears and the cut starts closing.

But how does your body even know where the damage is?

Why does a scab form, and what's happening underneath it?

Where does the new skin come from?

And why does a healing cut sometimes itch?

Your body is doing a lot more repair work than you can see.

And even when the cut looks healed, it may not be finished yet.

Liked this one? Please rate the show on Apple Podcasts or Spotify. It helps other curious families find us.

Topic: Your Body · Published 13 August 2026

Talk about it after listening

  1. What do platelets do when you get a cut?
  2. Why is it a bad idea to pick a scab?
  3. Why does a healing cut sometimes feel itchy?

Two adult hosts. Lightly edited for reading; small differences from the audio are possible.

Host 1: Hello. Welcome to the podcast. Dad, how does it work? So picture this. You're riding along, you fall off your bike and you scrape your knee on the pavement.

Host 2: Oh, man, that is the worst.

Host 1: Right. You look down, you see a little blood and it just feels totally unfair. But then, you know, a few days later, a rough scab forms and eventually fresh skin appears right underneath it.

Host 2: Yeah, it's pretty amazing how that happens.

Host 1: It really is. Like, how does your body know exactly how to fix this hole without you ever giving it instructions?

Host 2: Well, it's basically magic, except, you know, it's biology. To really picture how it works, imagine your body as this incredibly busy, heavily guarded city.

Host 1: Okay, a busy city, I like that.

Host 2: And your skin is the thick protective outer wall surrounding that entire city. It keeps the useful stuff like water safely inside and it totally locks out the dirt and germs.

Host 1: Right, so when I scrape my knee, it's like a bulldozer just smashed a giant hole right through the city wall.

Host 2: Exactly. And obviously the city can't just leave a gaping hole there, but what's really fascinating is how the city actually finds out the wall is broken.

Host 1: Wait, yeah, how do they know? Because it's not like you have tiny security guards walking around with radios, right?

Host 2: No tiny radios. When the wall breaks, the skin cells that get crushed actually burst open. They spill their inside contents out into the surrounding area.

Host 1: Oh, wow.

Host 2: Yeah. And the surviving cells nearby sense that spilled material and they instantly recognize it as a chemical alarm.

Host 1: Oh, that makes perfect sense. So it's like a physical chain reaction. The broken cells literally trigger the alarm.

Host 2: Exactly. And that alarm process is also why it hurts so much.

Host 1: Right, because the pain isn't just your body being mean to you after you fall.

Host 2: No, not at all. It's a very loud warning signal from your brain yelling, hey, the wall is compromised. Stop dragging this knee on the concrete.

Host 1: Yeah, please stop.

Host 2: Exactly. Pain is highly protective. But while you're busy saying ouch, the body's first priority is stopping the leak. To do that, tiny puzzle pieces in your blood called platelets respond to those chemical alarms.

Host 1: Okay, platelets.

Host 2: Right, they rush into the hole and when they hit the rough broken edges of the cut, they activate. They get super sticky and clump together to form a temporary emergency patch.

Host 1: So they basically plug the hole from the inside.

Host 2: Yes, exactly.

Host 1: And when the outside air hits that sticky patch, it dries out and hardens into a scab.

Host 2: You got it.

Host 1: Now, I have to admit, when I was younger, I used to think the scab was the new skin. Like it just turned into skin. But that's not right, is it?

Host 2: No, no, not at all. The scab is just the dry crusty roof.

Host 1: Just a roof, okay.

Host 2: Yeah, think of it as a temporary tarp thrown over an active construction site. The real work is happening underneath.

Host 1: I love that. A secret construction site inside your knee.

Host 2: It really is.

Host 1: And I actually know a bit about what happens under there. Because, well, the hole was open to the outside world, so dirt and germs definitely got in.

Host 2: Oh yeah, inevitably.

Host 1: Right, so the city sends in the cleanup crew first, like your immune cells.

Host 2: Yep, they are the cleanup crew.

Host 1: They actually swallow up the dirt and broken cell pieces to clear the area, right?

Host 2: They do. And because those immune cells are working so hard, they increase the blood flow to that spot.

Host 1: Oh, is that why it gets red?

Host 2: Exactly. That is exactly why the area around a new cut might get a little red and warm to the touch. That's just the heat of the cleanup crew working.

Host 1: Okay, so once the site is cleaned up, the actual builders arrive and they start laying down a material called collagen.

Host 2: Right, collagen.

Host 1: It's this incredibly strong flexible protein that builds a support structure right across the bottom of the hole. It's a lot like rebuilding the sturdy wooden frame inside a house's wall before you put the nice smooth drywall and paint on the outside.

Host 2: That is a brilliant way to look at it. They have to fix the deep foundation first.

Host 1: But here is the thing that drives everyone crazy. Why does it get so incredibly itchy right at this stage of healing?

Host 2: Oh, the itch.

Host 1: It almost feels like a design flaw that your body triggers an itch that makes you want to ruin the repair job.

Host 2: It really does seem like a flaw, but there's a mechanical reason for it. As the new skin cells slide into place over that collagen frame, the skin is physically pulling and tightening to stretch across the gap.

Host 1: Oh, I see.

Host 2: Yeah, and at the same time, the little nerve endings that got damaged are finally waking back up.

Host 1: So they're confused.

Host 2: Sort of. Your brain misinterprets all that mechanical pulling and nerve tingling as an itch.

Host 1: Which makes you want to scratch.

Host 2: Exactly.

Host 1: But we should gently warn everyone listening. Never, ever pick the scab.

Host 2: Never. Keep your hands off the construction site.

Host 1: Right, because when you pick it, you are literally ripping the roof off the construction site before the workers are finished. You undo all their hard work and force them to start over.

Host 2: Yeah, you really don't want to do that. Now, once it is finally healed and the scab falls off naturally, you might notice a scar.

Host 1: Right, and it doesn't look quite like normal skin. Why can't the body just print normal skin again?

Host 2: Well, think about tearing a beautiful drawing in half. If you quickly tape it back together, the drawing is fixed, right?

Host 1: Yeah, it holds together perfectly well.

Host 2: Right, but you can still see that slightly shiny taped line exactly where the break was.

Host 1: Oh, I get it. A scar is that taped line.

Host 2: Exactly. Your body cared a lot more about closing the dangerous hole quickly to keep germs out than it did about making the skin look absolutely perfect.

Host 1: Safety first, decoration later.

Host 2: Always.

Host 1: So, closing a cut isn't just magic, it's millions of tiny city workers communicating through chemistry, cleaning up the mess, and rebuilding your protective wall.

Host 2: It's amazing when you put it all together, and you know, here is something really wild to think about.

Host 1: Oh, what's that?

Host 2: When babies are still growing inside their mothers, like before they are born, their skin can heal from cuts completely flawlessly, without any scars at all.

Host 1: Wait, really? No taped lines?

Host 2: None. The cellular builders just communicate differently early on. It really makes you wonder if scientists can figure out exactly how the body does that before we're born. Maybe one day they could teach our adult bodies how to heal without leaving a single scar.

Host 1: That is incredible to think about. If this sparked your curiosity and you wanna hear more episodes like this, make sure to follow our podcast to catch new episodes and updates.
