How Does Paper Even Come From Trees?

Listen now
Spotify (opens in a new tab)Apple Podcasts (opens in a new tab)Other apps (opens in a new tab)

You’re holding a sheet of paper, and it feels soft and easy to bend. But trees are hard. You can knock on them. They don’t fold.

So how did a tree turn into this thin paper in your hands?

If paper comes from trees, why can you rip it so easily? And why does paper get weak and fall apart when it gets wet?

It starts to feel less like magic once you realize what trees are actually made of. And what has to happen to them before they can ever become paper.

Quick answer: How Does Paper Even Come From Trees?

Paper is made from wood. Trees are chopped into chips, mixed with water and broken down into a soupy pulp of tiny fibres, which is spread thin, pressed and dried into sheets.

Topic: Home & Everyday Stuff · Published 19 February 2026

Talk about it after listening

  1. A tree is like a giant rope. What is it made of, if you zoom way in?
  2. Tear a piece of paper and look at the edge. What are the fuzzy bits?
  3. Why does paper fall apart when it gets wet?

Host 2: Oh, all the time.

Host 1: And you're really focused, you're pressing down hard with your colored pencil, and then snap, the tip breaks.

Host 2: Yes, it's so frustrating. And then sometimes you get so annoyed, you crumple up the paper and maybe you even tear it.

Host 1: Exactly, and that made me think about something. A tree, a tree is super hard, right? You can knock on it, it feels like a wall.

Host 2: Yeah, you can't bend it.

Host 1: You can't. But paper, which comes from trees, is, well, it's soft. You can fold it, you can tear it really easily.

Host 2: That is a weird thought. How does something so hard and strong like a tree trunk turn into something so thin and floppy like a sheet of paper? What's actually happening there?

Host 1: That is the perfect question. And to understand it, we have to imagine we have superpowered eyes and we're going to zoom in way, way in on a piece of wood.

Host 2: Okay, I'm zooming in.

Host 1: From the outside, it looks like one solid thing. But the secret is that a tree is actually a lot like a giant thick rope.

Host 2: A rope. But a rope is bendy and it's made of string.

Host 1: A rope feels solid if you pull on it, but you know it's really made of thousands of smaller threads all twisted together.

Host 2: Right.

Host 1: Well, a tree is the same way. It's made of millions and millions of tiny microscopic strands called fibers.

Host 2: Fibers?

Host 1: And just like in a rope, all those fibers are packed together super, super tightly. They run all the way up and down the tree and that tight bundle is what makes the tree so strong. It's why it's so hard to break a branch.

Host 2: You're trying to snap a whole bundle of threads at once.

Host 1: You got it. So if we want to make paper, the first thing we have to do is... Well, we have to stop the wood from acting like a rope. We need to unbundle all those threads.

Host 2: So how do you do that? You can't just untwist a tree.

Host 1: No, you can't. So what happens is the wood gets chopped up into tiny little pieces like wood chips. And then those chips get mixed with a lot of water and are cooked and softened. And that whole process, the water and the heat, it makes that tight bundle just unravel.

Host 2: Oh, so all the little threads start to come apart from each other?

Host 1: They start to separate and float away from each other. So instead of one big hard piece of wood, you now have millions of these tiny little fibers just swimming around in the water.

Host 2: So it's not solid anymore. It's mushy.

Host 1: It's very mushy. People in the paper-making business call it pulp, but a great way to think of it is like a soup. A soup of tiny wood threads.

Host 2: A soup? I like that. Okay, so we've taken our hard rope and turned it into a liquidy soup. But paper is a dry, flat sheet. How do you get from the soup to the sheet?

Host 1: Right, so you take the soup, which is mostly water with all the fibers floating in it, and you spread it out on a big flat screen, kind of like a window screen.

Host 2: And the water just drains out.

Host 1: The water drains away. And its job is really important. The water acts like a helper, spreading all those fibers out into a really thin, even layer.

Host 2: But I still don't get it. Why don't all the little threads just turn to dust when the water is gone? Is there some kind of glue you add?

Host 1: That's what you'd think, but no glue at all. This is the really clever part, because the fibers are long and thin. As they get left behind by the water, they start to overlap and crisscross.

Host 2: They just get tangled up with each other.

Host 1: Exactly. Imagine dumping a big pile of wet string on the floor. As it dries, it would just become a tangled mat.

Host 2: Okay, I can picture that.

Host 1: It's the same idea. As the last bit of water evaporates, the fibers get pulled really close together. And because they're all tangled up, they lock into place. They hold on to each other.

Host 2: So they basically make the sheet all by themselves, just by tangling.

Host 1: Precisely. They form a solid thin sheet just by drying in a tangled up web.

Host 2: And you can actually see the proof of this, can't you?

Host 1: You absolutely can. In fact, everyone listening can do a little experiment right now. If you have a piece of paper, just tear it a little bit.

Host 2: Okay, tearing a piece.

Host 1: Now look really, really closely at the edge you just tore. Yeah. Is it a perfectly sharp, clean line?

Host 2: No, not at all.

Host 1: Yeah.

Host 2: It's fuzzy. It's got all these little wispy bits sticking out.

Host 1: That fuzz. Those are the ends of the tiny fibers. When you tore the paper, you weren't breaking a solid thing. You were just pulling that tangle of threads apart.

Host 2: So I'm seeing the actual wood fibers.

Host 1: You're seeing the ends of them where they were pulled away from their neighbors.

Host 2: That makes so much sense. And it also explains something else. What happens when paper gets wet?

Host 1: Oh, it gets completely ruined. It turns to mush.

Host 2: Right. And now I think I know why. Is it because the water is getting back in?

Host 1: That is exactly it. The water sneaks back in between all those tangled fibers.

Host 2: Yeah.

Host 1: And it loosens their grip on each other.

Host 2: It lets them slide around again.

Host 1: It does. It starts turning the paper back into that soupy mush it came from.

Host 2: Yeah.

Host 1: The tangle starts to fall apart.

Host 2: That's why you can just poke your finger right through wet paper.

Host 1: Yep, and it's also why you can bend paper, but a stick just snaps. The stick is still a tight rope of fibers. It breaks. But in paper, the fibers are in a thin, flat sheet, so they have a little room to move and shift.

Host 2: So what about cardboard then? Is that just really, really thick paper?

Host 1: It's the exact same idea. It's just made with more layers of fibers pressed together to make it thicker and stronger. It's like stacking a few sheets of paper on top of each other.

Host 2: So when we really think about it, paper isn't a new thing they invented. It's just rearranged tree.

Host 1: That is the best way to put it. We're using the exact same material, the wood fibers. We haven't added anything magical. We've just changed the shape.

Host 2: We took a thick, tight rope...

Host 1: And we untangled it and then rearranged all those same threads into a thin flat layer. That's all paper is.

Host 2: So every drawing you make or every page in a book you read, it's actually just a flat network of millions of tiny little threads that used to be standing up straight and tall in a forest.

Host 1: A tiny flat forest in your hands.

Host 2: That's a pretty amazing thought.

Host 1: It really is.
