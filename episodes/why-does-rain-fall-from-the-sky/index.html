Why Does Rain Fall From the Sky?

Listen now
Spotify (opens in a new tab)Apple Podcasts (opens in a new tab)Other apps (opens in a new tab)

You’re outside playing, and suddenly it starts raining. You look up at the sky and think, wait… where did all that water come from?

Did someone pour it from the clouds? Was it hiding up there the whole time?

You’ve seen puddles dry. You’ve seen steam rise from hot water.

So how does water go up into the air…and then come back down as rain?

Quick answer: Why Does Rain Fall From the Sky?

The Sun warms water in seas and puddles until it rises into the air as invisible vapour. High up it cools and forms tiny droplets that make clouds, and when the droplets join into drops too heavy to float, they fall as rain.

Topic: Weather & Earth · Published 16 February 2026

Talk about it after listening

  1. When soup steam hits a cold pot lid, it turns back into drops. What is the "pot", the "stove" and the "lid" for our whole planet?
  2. Every raindrop starts around something tiny floating in the air. Can you name two of those tiny things?
  3. Why do tiny cloud droplets float, but a raindrop falls? How many droplets does it take to make one raindrop?

Host 1: Perfect. So you see that steam rising up from the soup. That's water turning into an invisible gas.

Host 2: Evaporation.

Host 1: Exactly. Now what happens when that hot steam hits the cooler glass lid?

Host 2: It fogs up. It turns back into little water droplets on the lid.

Host 1: And if you leave it long enough.

Host 2: Those drops get bigger and bigger until they drip back down into the soup.

Host 1: Precisely. That little pot of soup is basically a miniature version of the earth. The earth is the pot, the sun is the stove, and the sky... Well, the sky is the lid.

Host 2: Ah, but that's where I get stuck. When I look up, I don't see a glass lid. What stops the water from just, you know, floating off into space forever?

Host 1: That's a fantastic question. And the thing is, there is a lid. It's just invisible. Really? Yeah. It's a layer in our atmosphere called the tropopause. It's a point where the air stops getting colder as you go up. And it acts just like that glass lid trapping all our weather and water down here with us.

Host 2: So we really are living inside a giant soup pot.

Host 1: In a way, yeah. It's a closed system. But to get from the ocean at the bottom of the pot all the way up to that lid, that's where the journey begins. That's evaporation.

Host 2: Okay, so I know evaporation is water turning into gas, but it's not that simple, is it? Water molecules, they like to stick together.

Host 1: They really do. They're very sticky. They're held together by these things called hydrogen bonds. It's like they're all holding hands in a big crowd and they don't want to let go.

Host 2: So for one of them to escape, it has to break that grip.

Host 1: Exactly. And to break a bond, you need energy. Lots of it. And that's where the sun comes in. The sun just blasts the ocean with heat and the molecules on the surface start absorbing it. They start to vibrate, just dance faster and faster.

Host 2: Until one of them gets enough of a kick to just launch into the air.

Host 1: It launches. It breaks free. But here's the part that is so cool, the part most people miss. When that molecule leaves, it takes that heat energy with it.

Host 2: It takes the heat with it.

Host 1: It does. I mean, think about when you get out of a swimming pool. Even on a hot day, you feel cold for a second.

Host 2: You start shivering immediately.

Host 1: That's the water evaporating off your skin. Yeah, and it's literally stealing your body heat to power its escape. It's called latent heat. The water molecule basically packs that heat into a suitcase and carries it up into the sky.

Host 2: So wait, the air isn't just humid. It's charged with stored energy.

Host 1: You've got it. That's the engine of our weather. A cloud isn't just a puff of water. It's a massive battery of solar energy that's been moved from the ocean's surface way up into the atmosphere.

Host 2: That completely changes how I think about a humid day. So, okay. This invisible energy-packed gas is rising up, but you always hear it gets colder the higher you go.

Host 1: It does.

Host 2: That's always confused me. Heat rises, so why is it freezing on top of a mountain?

Host 1: It's a great question. It's all about pressure. Down here the air is thick, it's dense. As you go up there's less air pressing down so the pressure drops and when a gas expands to a low pressure area it cools down.

Host 2: Okay so our little water molecule is rising, expanding, and getting really cold.

Host 1: And that brings us to step two. Condensation. This is the moment the invisible becomes visible. But there's a secret ingredient.

Host 2: A secret ingredient?

Host 1: Yep. Water vapor can't just decide to become a liquid drop again in mid-air. It's too clean. It needs a surface to form on.

Host 2: Like the glass lid on the pot.

Host 1: Exactly. But there's no giant sheet of glass in the sky, so it finds the next best thing.

Host 2: Which is what?

Host 1: Dust. Smoke from a fire. A tiny grain of salt from the ocean. Even microscopic bacteria. The air is full of these tiny floating particles.

Host 2: Hold on, are you saying every single raindrop forms around a tiny piece of dirt?

Host 1: Every single one. Without those little specks of dust, we would never get rain. It would just be a very humid, hazy sky. The cold water vapor finds a dust mote and clings to it, then another molecule joins and another. Wow.

Host 2: So a cloud isn't just water, it's a floating collection of wet dust.

Host 1: That's a beautiful way to think about it. And remember that suitcase full of heat?

Host 2: Yeah, the latent heat.

Host 1: When the vapor turns back into a liquid drop, it unpacks the suitcase, it releases that heat back into the air.

Host 2: And what does that do?

Host 1: It warms the air around the brand new droplet, which makes that air rise even faster, which then sucks up more moist air from below. It creates a chain reaction, a self-fueling engine. That's how you get those giant thunderheads that seem to just explode upwards.

Host 2: The cloud is literally feeding itself with the heat it [unclear] from the ocean. That is incredible. But we still have the gravity problem. Why don't these little wet dust motes just fall right away?

Host 1: Because they are unbelievably tiny. So small that even the slightest upward puff of air, an updraft, can keep them floating. It's like dust dancing in a sunbeam in your house. Gravity's pulling, sure, but the air currents are just stronger.

Host 2: So, the constant battle. Gravity pulling down, updrafts pushing up.

Host 1: It's a wrestling match. And for a long time, the updraft wins. But inside that cloud, it's chaos. These tiny droplets are zooming around and they start crashing into each other.

Host 2: A traffic accident in the sky.

Host 1: A merger. Two little droplets collide and become one bigger one, then that one hits another, and another. To get a single raindrop, the kind that actually feels wet when it hits you. Yeah. You need about a million of those tiny cloud droplets to all combine.

Host 2: A million?

Host 1: Serious. Around a million, yes. And once it gets that big and that heavy.

Host 2: The updraft can't hold it anymore.

Host 1: Gravity finally wins the match. The drop is too heavy and it falls. And that's rain.

Host 2: What a journey from liquid in the ocean, ripping itself free with the sun's heat, traveling miles up, finding a piece of dust, crashing into a million friends, and then falling all the way back down.

Host 1: And the trip doesn't even end there. It hits the ground, flows into a river back to the ocean, and the whole thing starts over.

Host 2: That actually reminds me of something I read. It sounded like a joke, the idea of dinosaur water. Is that real?

Host 1: Yes. It sounds like a myth, but it's absolutely true. Statistically, it's almost certain.

Host 2: Okay, walk me through that. How am I possibly drinking the same water as a T-Rex?

Host 1: Well, think about that invisible lid, the closed system. Earth isn't getting new water deliveries from space. We have the same amount of water now that we had billions of years ago.

Host 2: So it's just been recycled again and again and again.

Host 1: Endlessly. The water in your glass right now could have been locked in a glacier during the ice age. Before that, it might have been inside a prehistoric plant. And yes, there's a good chance it passed through a dinosaur at some point.

Host 2: That is both disgusting and absolutely amazing at the same time.

Host 1: It connects us. But here's another thought. That cycle doesn't always move at the same speed.

Host 2: What do you mean?

Host 1: A water molecule evaporates, goes up into a cloud. How long do you think it stays up there before it rains down?

Host 2: A few weeks, maybe a month?

Host 1: On average, only about nine days.

Host 2: Nine days? That's it?

Host 1: It's a quick turnaround. But once that drop falls into the deep ocean, it might stay there for 3,000 years before it gets another chance to evaporate.

Host 2: Wow, 3,000 years.

Host 1: And if it lands in Antarctica and becomes part of an ice sheet, it can be locked away for hundreds of thousands of years. So most of our planet's water is just resting. The rain we see is just the tiny active fraction.

Host 2: Which makes you realize how important that active part is. If that cycle just stops...

Host 1: If the engine stopped, the rivers would drain into the sea and never be refilled. The land would become a desert. All life on land depends on that elevator constantly moving water up and bringing it back down.

Host 2: It's like the planet's circulatory system.

Host 1: That's a perfect way to put it. It moves heat around, it waters the continents, it keeps the whole system in balance.

Host 2: So what about something like snow? Is that a completely different process?

Host 1: Nope, same recipe, just a different temperature. Remember the little dust particle? Yeah. If the air way up there is below freezing, the water vapor doesn't bother turning into a liquid first. It goes straight from a gas to a solid. It forms an ice crystal directly on that dust mote.

Host 2: Skips the liquid step entirely.

Host 1: It skips it. And that's what creates that beautiful six-sided shape of a snowflake.

Host 2: And hail. Hail feels angrier than rain or snow.

Host 1: Hail is when that wrestling match between gravity and the updraft gets really intense. A drop freezes, starts to fall, but a powerful updraft blasts it back up high into the cloud.

Host 2: Like a ping pong ball.

Host 1: Exactly. It gets another coating of water which freezes making it bigger, falls, it's blown back up again. It just keeps collecting layers of ice like an onion. Eventually gets so heavy nothing can hold it and it just plummets.

Host 2: So if you cut a hailstone in half you can actually see the rings from its trips up and down.

Host 1: You can. It's a little history of its violent journey inside the storm.
