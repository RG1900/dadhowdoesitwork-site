How Did the Dinosaurs Disappear?

Listen now
Spotify (opens in a new tab)Apple Podcasts (opens in a new tab)Other apps (opens in a new tab)

You look up at a giant dinosaur skeleton and wonder how something so powerful could vanish.

Did every dinosaur suddenly die at the same time?

And how could one asteroid cause trouble across the entire planet?

The strange part is that it didn’t need to hit every dinosaur.

Something happened to the sunlight.

Then the plants began to struggle, food became scarce, and being enormous stopped being helpful.

But if all the dinosaurs disappeared, why are birds still here?

Maybe some dinosaurs never really left.

Quick answer: How Did the Dinosaurs Disappear?

About 66 million years ago, a giant rock from space, an asteroid, crashed into Earth. The crash sent dust and smoke into the sky that blocked sunlight and changed the climate, and most dinosaurs couldn't survive. Birds are the dinosaurs that did.

Topic: Animals & Nature · Published 17 July 2026

Talk about it after listening

  1. How big was the asteroid, and where did it hit?
  2. What was the "sunlight curtain", and why did it hurt the plants first?
  3. Why was being huge a problem for dinosaurs? Which dinosaurs are still around today?

Host 2: Wait, three-quarters of everything?

Host 2: Wow. So this wasn't just bad luck for the dinosaurs. This was like a huge planet-wide reset.

Host 2: Like an asteroid?

Host 2: A whole city, like, falling from space?

Host 1: Yeah. About six miles wide and traveling incredibly fast. It crashed into what is now Mexico, leaving this giant bowl-shaped scar called a crater.

Host 2: Okay, a city-sized rock crashing into the Earth. That sounds unimaginably powerful.

Host 1: Oh, the heat was just intense. When it hit, it created massive earthquakes, huge waves on the oceans, and fires all around that area.

Host 2: Okay, but here is where I want to push back a little bit.

Host 1: Sure. That sounds absolutely terrible for any animal living right there in Mexico. But the Earth is huge.

Host 2: It is.

Host 1: Why would a dinosaur living, say, thousands of miles away, even care about a rock hitting just one spot?

Host 2: That is the perfect question to ask Because if it was just a big explosion, animals on the other side of the planet would have been fine.

Host 1: Right? The fire wouldn't reach them.

Host 2: Exactly. But the crash caused something much deadlier. I want you to picture something we'll call the sunlight curtain.

Host 1: The sunlight curtain? What does that mean?

Host 2: Well, the crash was so powerful that it blasted huge amounts of shattered rock, soot and dust high, high up into the sky.

Host 1: Like, up into the clouds?

Host 2: Even higher. It went way up into the atmosphere where there is no rain to wash it back down.

Host 2: Yes. The wind spread this dust all the way around the entire planet. It stayed up there, blocking a huge amount of sunlight from reaching the ground.

Host 1: So the whole world just goes dark.

Host 2: Yeah, exactly. As if someone had pulled a heavy dark curtain over the whole planet.

Host 1: Man, that sounds terrifying.

Host 2: It really was.

Host 2: Oh, well, it would get really weak and die.

Host 1: Exactly. And that happened all over the world to plants and even to tiny microscopic living things in the ocean that need sunlight.

Host 2: Okay, so if the plants die, then the plant-eating dinosaurs have absolutely nothing to eat.

Host 1: Right, they start to starve.

Host 2: And then the meat-eating dinosaurs who eat those guys also have nothing to hunt. So it's like pulling away the bottom pieces of a giant tower.

Host 1: That is a brilliant way to put it. The asteroid didn't need to hit the dinosaurs directly. It just knocked out the bottom of their food web.

Host 2: Because the plants were the foundation.

Host 1: Exactly. Plus, with the sun blocked, the world got incredibly cold and habitats were just ruined.

Host 2: Okay, but let's do a quick thought experiment. If we had to guess which animals would survive a dark freezing disaster, wouldn't we choose the biggest, strongest ones? Like the T-Rex?

Host 1: But in this case, being huge suddenly became a terrible disadvantage.

Host 2: Wait, really? Why?

Host 1: Because a giant animal needs a massive amount of food every single day just to stay alive.

Host 2: Oh, right.

Host 1: So when the plants died, the big animals simply couldn't find enough food to survive. They starved.

Host 2: So that's how the small animals made it. Because a tiny frog or a little mammal needs way less food, right?

Host 1: Exactly. Smaller animals needed much less. Plus they could hide.

Host 2: Hide where?

Host 1: In burrows underground. Or safely underwater away from the freezing cold.

Host 2: Wow, okay. But they still had to eat something.

Host 1: Right. And the best part is they could eat things that didn't need fresh sunlight, like dead leaves, insects, or seeds that were safely buried in the dirt.

Host 2: It wasn't a contest of strength at all. It was about who could adapt and survive on less.

Host 1: Now I should add that the asteroid wasn't the absolute only thing going wrong.

Host 2: Oh, there was more?

Host 1: Yeah, over in what is now India, there were massive cracks in the ground releasing a lot of lava and gases.

Host 2: Giant volcanoes?

Host 1: Basically, yeah. This volcano activity had already made the world a bit tougher to live in. But the asteroid and that sunlight curtain was the main reason for the sudden disaster.

Host 2: Okay, so this brings up my biggest question. Did all the dinosaurs actually disappear?

Host 1: Well, I want you to think about a pigeon hopping on the sidewalk outside a cafe.

Host 2: Okay, I'm picturing a pigeon.

Host 1: Think about its body. It has two legs, feathers, wings, and a beak. Birds didn't just come along after the dinosaurs. They actually are a surviving branch of the dinosaur family.

Host 2: Wait, a pigeon is a living dinosaur?

Host 1: It really is.

Host 2: That is blowing my mind right now.

Host 1: I know. While the huge dinosaurs died, some small, feathered dinosaurs managed to survive.

Host 2: Let me guess, because they were small.

Host 1: You got it. Their small bodies meant they needed less food. And those beaks were perfect for cracking open the seeds hiding in the dirt.

Host 2: Wow, the seeds that survived while the sunlight curtain was closed.

Host 1: Precisely. Now, just to clarify, extinction means every single member of a family dies. While most of the dinosaur branches went completely extinct, the bird branch made it through and evolved into all the amazing birds we see today.

Host 2: Okay, so if you ask where all the mighty dinosaurs went, the honest answer is that most of them died when the sunlight curtain closed.

Host 1: Yes, that's true.

Host 2: But some of them never went anywhere at all and are still flying right over our heads.

Host 1: Survival isn't always about being the biggest or the toughest. Sometimes it's about being flexible and knowing how to make do with less.
