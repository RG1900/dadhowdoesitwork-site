Why Isn't There Wi-Fi Outside?

Listen now
Spotify (opens in a new tab)Apple Podcasts (opens in a new tab)Watch on YouTube (opens in a new tab)Other apps (opens in a new tab)

At home, your Wi-Fi is always there.

Then you walk outside, and it suddenly disappears.

Where did it go?

Did the internet stay inside your house?

You might find a small box at home called a router.

That box creates the Wi-Fi around you.

When you leave, you leave the router behind.

So your phone has to connect another way to reach the internet.

Two adult hosts. Lightly edited for reading; small differences from the audio are possible.

Host 2: Hello, welcome to the podcast. Dad, how does it work?

Host 1: It is great to be back.

Host 2: So I was sitting on the couch the other night, you know, end of the week, just relaxing. I grabbed the tablet, tapped a movie I wanted to watch and poof, it just started playing.

Host 1: Ah, the best feeling. Instant movie night.

Host 2: It is. But then I kind of stopped and just looked at the tablet. It's just a piece of glass in my hands. There are no wires plugged into it, nothing. And it felt like magic, like the movie was just floating in the air around me and I just reached out and grabbed it. But that's not real, right? It can't be magic.

Host 1: It definitely feels like it. And that's on purpose, you know, it's designed to be that easy. But you're right, it's not magic. It's actually, I think, something way cooler. It involves invisible waves, but also blinking lights and, believe it or not, giant cables at the very bottom of the ocean.

Host 2: Okay, you lost me at the ocean part. That sounds wild. Let's start back here in the living room. If it's not magic in the air, how does that movie actually get to my screen?

Host 1: To figure that out, we have to find something that's probably hiding in your house right now. So, for everyone listening, just kind of look around the room you're in. You're looking for a small box.

Host 2: A box, like a cardboard box.

Host 1: No, not cardboard. Usually plastic. It's often black or white. It'll have little blinking lights on it. Green ones, maybe orange. And sometimes it has these little antennas sticking up. Sort of like robot ears.

Host 2: Ooh, I know exactly what you're talking about. The one that's usually behind the TV that we're told never to unplug.

Host 1: That's the one. That little box is called a router and it is the single most important piece of the puzzle for your home internet.

Host 2: The router. Okay, so is the internet inside that box? Like is the whole movie just shrunk down and living in there?

Host 1: Mmm, no, not quite. The router is more of a helper. A very important one. The best way to think about it is like a gate or maybe a doorway for your house.

Host 2: A doorway?

Host 1: Yeah, imagine your house has a front door, right? You keep it locked. Strangers can't just wander in. The internet is like this giant, busy, chaotic world outside your house, and the router... the router is the security guard standing at the gate.

Host 2: So it decides what comes in and out.

Host 1: Precisely. Nothing, and I mean nothing, from the internet gets to your tablet or your computer or your game console without passing through that little box first. It's the bridge between your house and, well, the entire world.

Host 2: Wait, my tablet isn't plugged into the box? I'm on the couch and the box is way over there behind a plant. How are they even talking to each other?

Host 1: Ah, okay, so that's the part that feels like magic. We call it Wi-Fi. But here's the big secret. Wi-Fi isn't the internet.

Host 2: It's not. I thought it was the same thing.

Host 1: Nope. Not at all. Wi-Fi is just an invisible wire. Imagine you and I are on opposite sides of a room and I toss a tennis ball to you. You can't see anything connecting us, but the ball travels through the air, right?

Host 2: Right.

Host 1: Wi-Fi is just like that. Your tablet and the router are constantly throwing these little invisible balls of information back and forth to each other.

Host 2: Okay, that makes sense. So walk me through it. I tap the video on my screen. What's the very next thing that happens?

Host 1: The second you tap, your tablet sends an invisible message, a shout, across the room to the router. It basically says, hey, gatekeeper, open up, I want to watch this movie.

Host 2: So it's asking for permission?

Host 1: It's asking for help. The tablet can't reach the outside world by itself, you know, it's stuck inside the house. Only the router is physically plugged into the wall, and that wire is what leads outside. So the router hears the request and says, got it, I'm on it.

Host 2: And then what?

Host 1: Then the router takes that request and sends it out of your house through the cables on the wall, down the street, and into this huge massive network we call the internet.

Host 2: Whoa, so my little tap on the screen actually sends a messenger out of my house?

Host 1: You got it. And that messenger travels incredibly fast. It goes out and finds these giant computers, maybe in another city, maybe even another country, where the movie is stored. Finds it, grabs it, and starts bringing it back.

Host 2: And it has to come back through the box?

Host 1: Every single tiny piece of it. It travels all the way back from wherever it was, through the cable, into your router. The router catches it, turns around, and then throws it wirelessly through the air to your tablet. And that whole round trip happens in less than a blink of an eye.

Host 2: That is actually unbelievable. I really did think the internet was just, you know, everywhere, like air. But you're saying it's a real physical journey.

Host 1: It is totally physical. We just don't see it. But outside your house, the internet is made of real stuff. Big, windowless buildings full of humming computers. Thick, black cables buried under the sidewalk. And yeah, like I mentioned before, cables as thick as your arm lying on the sand at the bottom of the ocean connecting whole continents.

Host 2: I still can't get over that. So if I'm watching a video from someone in, say, Japan, that signal literally went underwater.

Host 1: It literally swam through a protected cable right on the ocean floor.

Host 2: Wow. Doesn't it, I don't know, get wet?

Host 1: It's got a really thick waterproof coating, but it's down there. Sometimes sharks even get curious and try to bite them.

Host 2: No way. A shark could be biting my internet connection?

Host 1: It happens. Yeah. But usually the signal just zips right past, comes up onto a beach somewhere, travels across the land, goes into your wall, into that blinking box, and then makes the final leap through the air to your hands.

Host 2: Okay, I have a question about how the movie gets here. You said it comes through the cable. Does the whole movie just squeeze through the wire at once? Like one giant block of movie?

Host 1: That's a great way to picture it, but no.

Host 2: Yeah.

Host 1: That would be like trying to push a whole watermelon through a tiny straw. It would get completely stuck.

Host 2: It would explode for sure.

Host 1: Right. So to keep the internet from getting all clogged up, it does something really clever. Imagine you built a giant Lego castle and you want to mail it to your friend. You can't just put the whole castle in a box, right?

Host 2: It would break. I'd have to take it apart first.

Host 1: Exactly. You break it down into all the little individual bricks. The internet does the exact same thing to your movie. It chops it up into thousands, sometimes millions of tiny little pieces, little digital bricks.

Host 2: Okay, tiny pieces.

Host 1: And here's another way to think about it that might help. Imagine all those tiny pieces are cars. And all the cables in the world are a giant network of roads and highways.

Host 2: A highway full of movie cars. I like it.

Host 1: Your movie isn't one big semi-truck. It's a thousand tiny sports cars. All racing from the computer where the movie lives, directly to your house.

Host 2: Do they all take the same road to get here?

Host 1: Not always. And that's the really cool part. If one road has a traffic jam, some of the cars might take a detour. They can go left, right, take different routes, but they're all heading for the same destination, your house.

Host 2: So my movie is literally a traffic jam of tiny cars racing to get to me.

Host 1: Pretty much. And guess who's standing at the end of your driveway directing traffic?

Host 2: The blinking box. The router.

Host 1: The router is the traffic controller. It sees all these little cars arriving, sometimes out of order, and it catches them. It checks to make sure they're for you, not your neighbor, and then it lines them all up in the perfect order. One, two, three, four.

Host 2: Because if they were in the wrong order, the movie would be all jumbled up.

Host 1: A total mess. The end might play before the beginning. The router's job is to reassemble the puzzle perfectly. It snaps all those Lego bricks back together and sends them to your tablet so fast you don't even notice. You just see a smooth video.

Host 2: That is so much work for that little box. It's sorting thousands of things a second.

Host 1: It's working incredibly hard. And that's why sometimes, you know, if everyone in the house is using the internet at once, maybe you're watching a movie, your brother's playing a game online, your mom's on video call, the router gets a little overwhelmed.

Host 2: It's too many cars in the driveway at once.

Host 1: Exactly. The traffic control just can't keep up, and that's when your video pauses and you get the little spinning circle. That's just the router saying, hold on, give me a second, I'm still sorting the cars.

Host 2: That makes so much sense. I always get annoyed at the spinning circle, but now I kind of feel bad for the router.

Host 1: It's doing its best.

Host 2: Okay, so that's the house. But here's another mystery for you. I don't always stay home. I get in the car, go to the park, and my phone still works. But the router is back at the house, plugged into the wall. How...

Host 1: That's a great question. You've left the range of your little invisible wire. You've gone too far from the gate.

Host 2: So is there like a giant router in the sky that follows me around?

Host 1: Not in the sky, no. But next time you're in the car, look out the window. You ever see those really tall metal towers?

Host 2: Yeah.

Host 1: Sometimes they look like big metal skeletons with little white panels on top.

Host 2: Oh, yeah, I see those everywhere. Sometimes they try to disguise them as really fake-looking pine trees.

Host 1: Exactly. Those are cell towers. And they are the secret to internet on the go.

Host 2: So when I leave my house, my phone just switches over to one of those?

Host 1: It does. Your phone is smart. It realizes, hey, I can't hear the home router anymore. It's gone quiet. So it starts looking for a new connection. It sends out a much stronger signal looking for the nearest tallest tower it can find.

Host 2: And the tower hears it.

Host 1: The tower is huge and powerful. It can hear your phone's little shout from far away. The tower basically answers back and says, I got you. I'll take your messages now.

Host 2: So the tower becomes my new router.

Host 1: In a way, yes. The tower is also plugged into the ground with a huge cable. So when you asked for a video at the park, your phone sends the request up to the tower and the tower sends it down into the ground into the same massive network of cables we were talking about before.

Host 2: So it all ends up in the cables eventually.

Host 1: It always ends up in the cables. The wireless part is usually just the very last step of the journey to get the signal into your hands.

Host 2: That explains so much. Speaking of traveling, this brings me to something my parents always say on vacation.

Host 1: Oh yeah.

Host 2: Whenever we go to a different country, my dad gets all serious and he's like, do not use the internet on your phone here, turn it off, it's expensive. Why? It's the same internet, right? Why does it suddenly cost more money?

Host 1: That is an excellent question. It all comes down to who owns the towers.

Host 2: Owns them?

Host 1: Yeah. So back home, your parents pay a phone company every month, right? Let's call them Blue Company. Blue Company built and owns all the cell towers in your country. Since parents pay them, you get to use their towers. It's part of the deal.

Host 2: Okay, so we're on Team Blue. Got it.

Host 1: Right. But when you get on a plane and fly to another country, does Blue Company have towers there?

Host 2: I'm guessing not. It's a whole different country.

Host 1: Exactly. They have no towers there at all. But a different company, let's call them Red Company, does. They built all the towers in that country.

Host 2: So my phone connects to a Red Company tower instead.

Host 1: It has to if you want it to work. But here's the catch. Your parents don't pay Red Company. So when your phone connects, the Red Company tower sees you're a visitor. It sees you belong to Blue Company from far away.

Host 2: Uh-oh.

Host 1: So Red Company basically makes a phone call to Blue Company and says, hey, one of your customers is over here using our tower. We'll let them, but you have to pay us for it.

Host 2: Ah, I see where this is going.

Host 1: It's like a toll road. You know how some highways, you have to pay money to drive on them?

Host 2: Yeah, you have to stop and throw coins in a basket.

Host 1: Exactly. At home, you're driving on your own free local roads. But when you travel, you're suddenly driving on someone else's private toll road. Every picture you send, every video you load, it's like driving through another toll booth. Cha-ching.

Host 2: So the local company charges my home company.

Host 1: And then your home company turns around and puts that charge on your parents' bill.

Host 2: And that's why my dad says to turn it off.

Host 1: That is exactly why. Because borrowing someone else's network can be very, very expensive.

Host 2: Wow. Okay, let me see if I've got this all straight. The biggest thing for me is, Wi-Fi is not the internet.

Host 1: Correct. That's the main takeaway.

Host 2: Wi-Fi is just the invisible connection between my tablet and the router.

Host 1: The invisible wire, yep.

Host 2: The router is the gatekeeper. It's the box that connects my house to the real internet.

Host 1: You got it.

Host 2: And the real internet is not a cloud, it's a physical thing. Cables under the ground and under the ocean.

Host 1: A giant real-world machine.

Host 2: And when I'm away from home, my phone talks to a cell tower, but when I'm in another country, it's like driving on a toll road and that costs extra money.

Host 1: That's the whole journey right there, from your finger to the router to the bottom of the ocean and back again.

Host 2: It's amazing. I don't think I'll ever look at that blinking box in the corner the same way again. Next time I see that spinning circle, I'm just going to picture all those little cars trying to get through a traffic jam to reach me.

Host 1: And maybe give the little box a mental thank you. It's working hard.

Host 2: If this sparked your curiosity and you want to hear more episodes like this, make sure to follow our podcast to catch new episodes and updates.
