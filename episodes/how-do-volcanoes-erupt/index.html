How Do Volcanoes Erupt?

Ages 5–10 | Volcanoes erupt when rising magma and expanding gas escape through openings in Earth's crust. Discover why some eruptions pour out lava while others blast out ash. Find out how fizzy water helps explain growing gas bubbles. Try this at home: Compare bubbles in open cups of still and fizzy water. Where was the gas before the bubbles appeared? Website, all episodes and transcripts: https://dadhowdoesitwork.com/Listen everywhere: https://pod.link/1877456012

Quick answer: How Do Volcanoes Erupt?

Deep underground, rock can melt into magma. Gas dissolved in that magma expands as it rises — like bubbles in fizzy water — and the mix pushes up through weak spots in Earth's crust; that's an eruption. Thick sticky magma traps more gas and tends to explode as ash, while runnier magma lets gas escape more gently and pours out as lava.

Topic: Weather & Earth · Published 6 November 2026

Talk about it after listening

  1. Deep underground, magma is a little less dense than the rock around it. What does that make the magma want to do, and what trapped gas acts like an engine to push it up faster?
  2. How is rising magma like a sealed bottle of fizzy water — and how is it different, since a mountain has no screw cap to unscrew?
  3. Why can runny magma ooze out as a smooth lava flow, while thick sticky magma is more likely to blast out as ash instead of smoke?

Two adult hosts. Lightly edited for reading; small differences from the audio are possible.

Host 2: Hello. Welcome to the podcast. Dad, How Does It Work? Today, we are taking a close look at something incredibly fascinating. Have you ever watched a video of a volcano erupting? And with that glowing orange material just, you know, flowing down a solid-looking mountain?

Host 1: Oh, yeah. It looks exactly like a river of fire.

Host 2: Exactly. And seeing that makes it so natural to assume the whole inside of the Earth is just this giant swirling ocean of liquid fire, just waiting to spill out.

Host 1: Right. That is the classic image. But the reality miles below our feet is entirely different. And honestly, I think it's a lot more interesting.

Host 2: Yeah, because we really need a reality check here. The Earth isn't just sloshing around with liquid rock. The vast majority of our planet is solid. That glowing orange magma, it really only forms in very specific pockets.

Host 1: Exactly. It forms where conditions are just perfect to cause partial melting. So the solid rock melts and mixes with crystals and all these dissolved gases.

Host 2: But this is where the physics kind of tripped me up. We're talking about magma that is miles underground, right? So you have miles of incredibly heavy solid rock pressing down on it. How does dense melted rock move upward? I mean, shouldn't all that weight basically just lock it in place?

Host 1: Well, the squeezing is definitely intense. But the magma is actually, it's slightly less dense than the solid rock around it.

Host 2: I mean, so it's lighter.

Host 1: Basically, yeah, it has less mass taking up the exact same space. Because of that tiny difference, it naturally wants to rise. So it wedges itself into tiny existing cracks in the rock above it.

Host 2: Okay, so it's wedging upward, but just being slightly less dense. I mean, that doesn't seem like enough force to trigger a massive mountain-blowing eruption.

Host 1: No, you're totally right. You really need an engine to drive that momentum. And that engine is the dissolved gas trapped inside the magma. Mostly just water vapor, actually.

Host 2: Okay.

Host 1: So think about a bottle of fizzy water. When it's sealed tight, the high pressure keeps all the carbonation totally dissolved in the liquid. You can't even see a single bubble. Deep underground, the immense weight of miles of overlying rock creates that exact same extreme pressure, forcing gases to stay entirely dissolved inside the magma.

Host 2: Okay, but here's where the fizzy water analogy falls apart for me. With a bottle, you need a person to literally unscrew the cap to release the pressure. There is no giant screw cap on top of a mountain. Nobody is shaking it or twisting off a lid.

Host 1: And that is the absolute beauty of this mechanism. There is no cap. The pressure drops simply because the magma is moving higher.

Host 2: Oh, because there is less rock above it.

Host 1: Exactly. As that magma naturally migrates higher through those cracks, there's literally just less rock pressing down. Less weight means the pressure naturally drops.

Host 2: Oh, wow.

Host 1: Yeah. And as that pressure falls, the dissolved gas finally separates and forms bubbles. As they rise, those bubbles expand, physically pushing the magma and forcing it up faster and faster.

Host 2: That is wild. A microscopic gas bubble expanding is what drives thousands of tons of rock to the surface.

Host 1: And once it breaches the surface, that's when we start calling it lava.

Host 2: Right. Magma below, lava above.

Host 1: But the way it actually erupts comes down to viscosity, or basically how sticky the magma is.

Host 2: Okay, so what if it's really runny?

Host 1: Well, if it's runny, those expanding gas bubbles just easily travel through the fluid and escape at the surface. The pressure vents naturally, which gives you a smooth, oozing lava flow.

Host 2: But what if the magma is incredibly thick and sticky?

Host 1: Then the bubbles get trapped. The gas keeps expanding because the pressure is dropping, but the sticky fluid won't let it escape.

Host 2: So the internal pressure is just building and building?

Host 1: Right, until the pressure of those trapped bubbles literally overcomes the physical strength of the rock itself. It violently shatters the magma.

Host 2: Whoa. Which means that towering dark gray cloud we see during big eruptions isn't actually smoke?

Host 1: No, not at all. It's volcanic ash. It is billions of razor-sharp fragments of shattered rock and volcanic glass. Blasted straight into the atmosphere.

Host 2: But wait, if the pressure is intense enough to shatter the rock like that, how do we end up with perfectly cone-shaped mountains? Like, an explosion shouldn't leave behind a neat structure.

Host 1: Yeah, we look at a volcano and assume the mountain was always there acting like a chimney. But in reality, lava and ash usually start emerging from totally flat ground or just long cracks.

Host 2: Really?

Host 1: Yep. Over thousands of years, those oozy lava flows cool into hard rock, and the shattered ash fragments settle back down. Eruption after eruption, they just pile up layer by layer.

Host 2: Oh, wow. So the violent eruption itself is literally what constructs the mountain?

Host 1: Exactly. The destruction is the construction.

Host 2: That is just such a wild journey. Going from solid rock, melting in deep pockets, rising just because it's less dense, and being driven to the surface entirely by falling pressure and expanding bubbles.

Host 1: It really highlights how dynamic the ground beneath our feet is.

Host 2: It really does. Well, next time you look at a glass of fizzy water sitting safely on your kitchen counter, just watch the tiny bubbles forming. You don't have to shake it. You don't have to unscrew anything. Just consider how that invisible trapped gas, driven simply by a drop in pressure, is the exact same mechanism, powerful enough to blast solid rock into the sky and reshape our planet.
