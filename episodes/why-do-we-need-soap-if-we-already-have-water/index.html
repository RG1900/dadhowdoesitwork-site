Why Do We Need Soap If We Already Have Water?

Listen now
SpotifyApple PodcastsOther apps

You rinse greasy pizza fingers under the tap. The water runs over them, but they still feel slippery. Why can water wash away mud but struggle with oil? What does soap add that water is missing? Are the bubbles really doing the cleaning? Does soap have to kill every germ to get it off your hands? Somehow, soap helps water grab what it could not grab alone.

Published 28 September 2026

Talk about it after listening

  1. Why can't plain water wash pizza grease off your fingers?
  2. A soap molecule has two "hands". What does each hand like to hold?
  3. Do more bubbles mean the soap is cleaning better? What does the episode say?

Two adult hosts. Lightly edited for reading; small differences from the audio are possible.

Host 1: Hello. Welcome to the podcast. Dad, how does it work?

Host 2: Glad to be here.

Host 1: Have you ever, like, eaten a super greasy slice of pizza or maybe some really salty chips and then tried to clean your shiny fingers with just plain water?

Host 2: Oh, yeah. It just doesn't work.

Host 1: Right. You run them under the faucet, but they still feel, well, totally slippery. So today, we're doing a deep dive into the everyday chemistry of cleaning, exploring exactly how basic soap manages to outsmart that stubborn grease.

Host 2: It's incredibly frustrating when water fails, honestly. I mean, we expect it to wash everything away. It carries mud down the drain perfectly fine.

Host 1: Yeah, mud is easy.

Host 2: Exactly. But when it comes to grease, water acts like the dirt isn't even there. And that's because, well, oil and water just do not mix.

Host 1: They pretty much hate each other.

Host 2: Right. If you drop cooking oil into a glass of water, you know, it forms those little blobs that just float. You can shake the glass all you want, but they clump right back together.

Host 1: So the water just slides right past oily dirt, which totally explains why water is great for, like, loose dust or things that dissolve easily like sugar, but completely fails on pizza grease. So what is soap actually doing that water can't?

Host 2: Well, think of a single soap molecule as a tiny two-handed helper.

Host 1: Two-handed helper?

Host 2: Yeah. These invisible molecules are super clever because they have two very different ends, or hands. One hand absolutely loves water. Scientists call this the hydrophilic end.

Host 1: Okay, and since they have two hands, let me guess, the other hand hates water.

Host 2: You got it. The other hand strongly avoids water, but it loves to grab onto oil and grease. That's the hydrophobic end.

Host 1: Oh, I see.

Host 2: So because it can hold water in one hand and, you know, oil in the other, the soap acts as a bridge between two things that normally repel each other.

Host 1: Okay, so if we zoom in super close, picture a greasy fingerprint as, like, a giant landscape. Those little helpers arrive in the sink.

Host 2: Right.

Host 1: They push their oil-loving hydrophobic hands deep into the grease while keeping their water-loving hands up in the stream of running water.

Host 2: That's a perfect way to picture it.

Host 1: But wait, if the soap is already grabbing the oil on its own, why do we have to physically rub our hands together?

Host 2: Well, think about trying to move a giant sticky lump of dough. It's really tough to move all at once.

Host 1: Yeah, that makes sense.

Host 2: So when you rub your hands together, the friction actually breaks those large grease patches into tiny droplets. It frees them from the little folds in your skin.

Host 1: Oh, so that makes it easier for the soap molecules to surround the grease. They trap those broken-up droplets.

Host 2: Exactly. They surround the tiny grease droplets completely. All the oil-loving hands point inward, holding the grease, and the water-loving hands point outward toward the sink water.

Host 1: Like building a microscopic cage.

Host 2: Yeah, they form a tiny packet, which is actually called a micelle. And once that micelle forms, the running water just grabs the water-loving outside of the packet and washes the whole thing down the drain.

Host 1: Well, you know, it reminds me of washing dishes. If I smear cooking oil on a plate and just run water over it, it stays completely slick.

Host 2: Yep, plain water won't touch it.

Host 1: But the second I add dish soap and scrub, it forms those micelles and washes right off. Though I guess if I spilled sugar on a plate, plain water takes it away instantly since sugar dissolves.

Host 2: Exactly. Soap is specifically our tool for pulling away oils and sticky mixtures.

Host 1: So since those packets, the micelles, are completely invisible, is that why soap makes so much foam? You know, so we can actually see it working?

Host 2: No, that's actually a really common myth. Visible foam is just a side effect of air getting trapped.

Host 1: Wait, really?

Host 2: Yeah. It might help you see where you scrubbed, but the real cleaning machines are those invisible molecules. I mean, you can easily have a soap that cleans perfectly without making a huge mountain of bubbles.

Host 1: Okay, what about germs then? If soap isn't a magical bubble potion, is it acting like a tiny poison that kills every single germ on our hands?

Host 2: No, not at all. It's much more accurate to think of soap as a removal team.

Host 1: A removal team?

Host 2: Right. Germs love to cling to the natural oils and dirt on your skin. So when the soap loosens that oily dirt and traps it in micelles, the germs get locked in there too.

Host 1: Oh, and then they're simply rinsed down the drain.

Host 2: Exactly.

Host 1: Which completely explains the 20-second handwashing rule. It's not a magic countdown that activates the soap, it just physically takes that much time to scrub all the hiding places between your fingers and under your nails.

Host 2: Precisely. You just need enough time to reach everywhere. And this exact same two-handed mechanism applies everywhere, you know?

Host 1: Like with shampoo.

Host 2: Oh, absolutely. Shampoo lifts oils from your hair, dish soap cleans your plates, and laundry detergent pulls greasy dirt out of your clothes. They're all just slightly different teams of those tiny helpers, grabbing oil with one hand and water with the other.

Host 1: Here's something interesting to think about, though. If soap is so incredibly good at ripping away oils, what happens if we wash our hands too much? Are we actually stripping away our skin's natural armor?

Host 2: Oh, that's a fascinating point. It really makes you look at the sink a little differently.

Host 1: If this sparked your curiosity, and you want to hear more episodes like this, make sure to follow our podcast to catch new episodes and updates.
