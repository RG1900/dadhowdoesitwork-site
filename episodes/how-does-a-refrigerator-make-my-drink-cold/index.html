How Does a Refrigerator Make My Drink Cold?

Listen now
Spotify (opens in a new tab)Apple Podcasts (opens in a new tab)Other apps (opens in a new tab)

Ages 5–10 | A refrigerator moves heat from inside to the outside, which keeps your drink cold.

You put a warm bottle in the refrigerator and come back to cold water.

But no ice touched it.

Where did the warmth go?

And why can the outside of the fridge feel warm?

Something inside keeps picking up heat and carrying it away.

So what would happen if you left the door open to cool the kitchen?

Website, all episodes and transcripts: https://dadhowdoesitwork.com/

Quick answer: How Does a Refrigerator Make My Drink Cold?

A fridge doesn't add cold; it takes heat out. A special liquid flows through pipes inside the fridge, soaks up heat from your drink and the air, and carries it to the back of the fridge, where it is released into the kitchen. That's why the back or sides of a fridge feel warm.

Topic: Home & Everyday Stuff · Published 15 October 2026

Talk about it after listening

  1. Does a fridge make cold, or does it move something? What does it move?
  2. Why do the back or sides of a fridge feel warm?
  3. Would leaving the fridge door open cool down your kitchen on a hot day? Why not?

Host 2: Wait, so it's not just, like, blasting cold air from somewhere?

Host 2: Okay, so my water bottle gets cold simply because the fridge is evicting its heat.

Host 1: Exactly. And the vehicle for that eviction is a special fluid called a refrigerant. It travels in these sealed tubes.

Host 2: Oh, right. So it's kept completely separate from the food and the air inside.

Host 1: Yeah, you got it. So heat naturally flows from warmer things to cooler things, right?

Host 2: Right. So the heat from the warm bottle flows out into the cooler air inside the fridge.

Host 1: Yep. And then it transfers again into the even colder refrigerant tubing.

Host 2: Gotcha.

Host 1: Inside those tubes, the fluid absorbs that heat and undergoes a phase change. It evaporates from a liquid into a gas.

Host 2: Oh, you know what? That is just like when you wash your hands, right?

Host 1: Yeah, exactly like that.

Host 2: Because, like, as your hands air dry, the evaporating water absorbs heat from your skin, which is why your wet hands suddenly feel really chilly.

Host 1: That is a perfect analogy. The fluid in the fridge is doing that, just on a continuous closed loop.

Host 2: But wait, here is where I get stuck. To get rid of that captured heat, the fluid has to release it into the kitchen.

Host 1: Right.

Host 2: But my kitchen on a summer day is way warmer than the inside of the fridge. Heat doesn't just naturally flow uphill into a hotter room.

Host 1: No, it doesn't. And that is exactly why your fridge needs to be plugged into the wall. This brings us to the compressor.

Host 2: Oh, is that the low humming sound you hear sometimes?

Host 1: Yep, that is the compressor kicking on. It takes that gas, which is holding all that heat, and it violently squeezes it.

Host 2: So it's raising the pressure?

Host 1: Exactly. By forcing it into a much tighter space, the pressure and the temperature both shoot way up. It squeezes it until the gas is physically hotter than the air in your kitchen.

Host 2: Oh, wow. Okay, so because the gas is now extremely hot, the heat will naturally flow out of the tubes and into the cooler room.

Host 1: Precisely. And that right there is why those outer panels feel warm.

Host 2: That makes so much mechanical sense. So as it bleeds that heat off into the room, what happens to the gas?

Host 1: Well, it loses energy and condenses right back into a liquid state.

Host 2: All right, so now we have a high-pressure liquid. But to start over and absorb more heat from my groceries, it has to get cold again.

Host 1: Right. So it passes through this really narrow restriction, like a tiny expansion valve.

Host 2: Kind of like letting the air out of a tightly squeezed balloon.

Host 1: Exactly like that. Passing through that tiny space causes a massive, sudden drop in pressure.

Host 2: Okay.

Host 1: And that pressure drop actually allows the liquid to boil inside the fridge.

Host 2: Wait, boil? When you say boil, I instantly picture, like, a hot pot of pasta water rolling on a stove.

Host 1: I know, it's a huge misconception. We always associate boiling with heat, but boiling is just the threshold where liquid turns into a gas.

Host 2: Oh, I see.

Host 1: And that threshold depends on temperature, but it also depends heavily on pressure.

Host 2: So by dramatically lowering the pressure, you're taking away the invisible weight holding the liquid together.

Host 1: You nailed it. Because the pressure drops so incredibly low, this special fluid boils while it is freezing cold.

Host 2: That is amazing.

Host 1: Yeah, so it turns to gas, absorbs more heat from your water bottle, and the whole cycle just repeats.

Host 2: And I guess that's why fridges have all that thick insulation, right? To stop the kitchen heat from migrating back in?

Host 1: Exactly. And there are temperature sensors that just monitor things and only run the compressor when it's absolutely needed.

Host 2: Okay, so this actually brings up a thought experiment I've always wondered about.

Host 1: Oh, let's hear it.

Host 2: Say it's a sweltering summer afternoon. If I just leave the fridge door wide open, could I eventually cool down my whole kitchen?

Host 1: It is so tempting to try that. But if you trace the energy exchange we just talked about, leaving the door open would actually warm your kitchen up.

Host 2: Wait, really? Even though the air right in front of the open door feels so cold?

Host 1: Yeah, because remember, the fridge isn't making cold air. It's just relocating heat from inside the box to outside the box.

Host 2: So it just cancels out?

Host 1: Exactly. It's a zero-sum game for the room's temperature, except for one crucial detail.

Host 2: The electricity for the compressor?

Host 1: Yep. Running that compressor to squeeze the gas generates its own waste heat. So overall, the appliance is pumping more total heat into the room than it's taking out.

Host 2: Wow. Okay. So just to recap this whole discovery, the bottle loses its heat to the colder air inside. Then the refrigerant fluid absorbs that heat, carries it outside the box, and the compressor squeezes it until it's hot enough to release the energy into the room.

Host 1: That's it.

Host 2: A fridge doesn't make cold. It just gives heat a one-way ticket out.

Host 1: It is a brilliant manipulation of physics. Just a one-way door for thermal energy.

Host 2: That is so cool. And it makes you think, if you ran the whole thing backward, moving heat from the freezing air outside and dumping it inside, you could warm your house.

Host 1: Exactly. And spoiler alert, that is basically how modern home heat pumps work.
