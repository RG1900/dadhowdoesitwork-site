How Do Trees Talk to Each Other?

Listen now
Spotify (opens in a new tab)Apple Podcasts (opens in a new tab)Other apps (opens in a new tab)

You see one tree getting eaten by insects, while the tree next to it looks untouched. How could the second tree possibly know there’s danger nearby? Trees can’t talk, shout, or send messages. But they can release tiny signals into the air that other trees may detect. And underground, their roots can connect with huge networks of fungi. So are trees actually warning each other? Are they sharing things through the soil? And how much of this is really communication, rather than just trees reacting to what’s around them?

Quick answer: How Do Trees Talk to Each Other?

Trees can warn each other. When insects chew a leaf, the tree releases invisible chemical signals into the air, and nearby trees that 'smell' them start making substances that taste bad to the insects. Underground, roots and fungi also connect trees.

Topic: Animals & Nature · Published 4 September 2026

Talk about it after listening

  1. When a beetle bites a leaf, what does the tree let out into the air? How is it like the smell of cut grass?
  2. What is the trade between a tree and the tiny fungus threads around its roots?
  3. Are the trees being kind to each other on purpose? What does the episode say?

Host 2: Exactly like a scent. I mean, you can picture it by thinking about a freshly mowed lawn. Have you ever noticed how you can smell that cut grass all the way across the yard?

Host 2: It really is a brilliant defense mechanism because the tree doesn't even have to think about it. The chemical signal just triggers this automatic physical response, you know, almost like a reflex when the doctor taps your knee.

Host 1: Wait, but that brings up an obvious problem. Because if this system uses the air, isn't it totally dependent on the weather? Like, what if the wind happens to be blowing the wrong way?

Host 2: Oh, that's a great point. If the wind is blowing away from the neighbor tree, the defense system just fails.

Host 1: Really? So the warning just never arrives.

Host 2: Yeah, if you zoom way, way in beneath the soil, past all the regular dirt, you'll find these millions of ultra-thin threads of fungi.

Host 1: Fungi, like mushrooms.

Host 1: That sunny kitchen. I like that.

Host 1: And those fungal threads, they're so incredibly thin, right? Like, they can squeeze into microscopic spaces in the dirt that the tree's thicker roots just can't even reach.

Host 2: Precisely. So they make a trade. The tree gives the fungus some of its sugar and the fungus delivers water and minerals back in return.

Host 1: Oh wow. And because a single fungus network can connect to the roots of dozens of different trees at once, it becomes a physical channel for information too.

Host 2: Yep, you got it.

Host 1: So when that first tree is attacked by the beetles, its chemical stress signals can travel right through those fungal threads over to the neighboring tree.

Host 1: And that triggers the second tree to prepare its defenses, just like the air smells do, but safely underground where the wind can't mess it up.

Host 1: So it's really tempting to sort of picture trees as these friendly neighbors, you know, deliberately looking out for each other on some magical forest internet where they kindly feed their friends.

Host 1: Right, so it's not trying to be nice.

Host 2: No, not at all. It needs the trees to survive so it can keep getting its sugar. If the tree dies, the fungus loses its sunny kitchen.

Host 1: So it's moving chemicals based purely on where things are concentrated.

Host 2: Exactly. And where they're needed to just keep its food source alive.

Host 1: So nature is really just using automatic plumbing to keep the whole system running.

Host 2: Right, rather than trees making conscious decisions to be kind.

Host 1: But what this really means is that a forest isn't just a collection of lonely blocks of wood.

Host 2: Yeah, it behaves almost like a single interconnected superorganism.

Host 1: It's a busy living neighborhood, constantly buzzing with automatic signals and trades and chemical reactions.

Host 2: It's just without any spoken words. So the next time you're in the park and you see a chewed-up leaf, you are likely standing right in the middle of this vast chemical chatter, a silent warning signal moving through the air and right under your feet.
